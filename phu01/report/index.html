Summary

Figure 1. The investigation at a glance.

Source: Swedish Public Employment Service (AF) Analysis 2026:04 (K081, K082) and own calculation (sections 6.1, 6.2 and 6.5).

Sweden's Public Employment Service (Arbetsförmedlingen, AF) calculates that Sweden will have an additional labour shortfall of roughly 270,000 people by 2035 (270,972 in AF's own table appendix, base scenario). The shortfall is the difference between the labour demand AF projects to 2035 and the number of people who would be employed if nothing else changes. The investigation addresses how much of that shortfall agentic AI can help close, by letting the work be done by fewer people.

It cannot be answered with evidence. Agentic AI has only begun to see wider use during 2026. Essentially all measured effects, in Sweden and internationally, concern generative AI used in chat form only. They show no broad transformation of employment so far, but a weakened entry path into the labour market for young people in AI-exposed occupations. This is therefore a scenario investigation. The effect levels are the author's own assumptions, based on what is analysed in the report and reported and justified openly. The calculation should be updated as outcomes become measurable, at a recommended frequency of six months.

AI's greatest power is analysis, not administration. Agentic AI has most impact where large volumes of data can be gathered and analysed, so that patterns are discovered that people often do not see. This applies to casework and expert work alike, but also to areas such as care, where continuously collected data can give early signs of changes that need action. Documentation and administration is the part that is easiest to introduce first, but is assessed as not being the most important effect (section 3.2).

Headline results.

  • To cover the entire labour shortfall, the need for labour would on average need to fall by 5.6% compared with today's number of employees, if the capacity freed up can be used in the occupations where the shortfall exists. That figure follows directly from AF's and Statistics Sweden's (SCB) statistics and does not depend on any assumption about AI.

  • The three scenarios give a gross reduction in demand of 11.0%, 17.9% and 24.6% (Low, Mid, High), corresponding to roughly 532,000, 866,000 and 1,192,000 people. All three are clearly above the 5.6% break-even.

Figure 2. Reduced labour demand in 2035 under the three scenarios, compared with the reduction required to cover the shortfall.

Source: Own calculation (break-even per section 6.1, scenarios per table 6.2). Shortfall per AF Analysis 2026:04 (K081, K082).

  • Without mobility between occupations, the shortfall is covered to 82% in Low and 99% in Mid when the calculation is made across 46 occupational groups. In Low, about 48,000 people of shortfall then remain, mainly in care, health care, construction and services. The same calculation at AF's own level of detail, 147 occupational groups, covers 73% in Low and 95% in Mid, leaving roughly 74,000 and 15,000 people of shortfall respectively (section 6.5). In care's largest shortfall occupations, 14–16% is required; the Low assumption (10%) is therefore not enough, while the Mid assumption (20%) is. The assumption for care is tested separately in section 6.6.

  • Beyond the shortfall, labour capacity equivalent to roughly 261,000, 595,000 and 921,000 employees is freed up. The investigation calls this a surplus. In High this equals 19% of today's employees and 8.7% of the population. The surplus arises mainly in office, casework and expert occupations, where the shortfall is small. These figures are not a forecast of unemployment: how much of the capacity becomes fewer people employed depends on occupational mobility, new demand, new tasks and working hours (section 6.11).

  • If lower prices lead to increased demand (volume compensation), the effect is reduced. The main calculation assumes no volume compensation and is therefore an upper bound. How large the compensation will be cannot be determined with today's evidence; with 50% compensation, 89% of the shortfall in Mid is still covered (sections 6.7 and 7).

Conclusion. The question is not whether AI can free up enough labour capacity in aggregate, but whether it is freed up where the shortfall exists. Sweden can have a labour shortage and a labour surplus at the same time. The shortfall sits in care (37.5% of the entire shortfall); the freeing-up happens mainly in casework, offices and expert work. This makes occupational mobility, targeted AI use in care, and the question of how a surplus should be managed if it materialises (working hours, retirement age, financing and the tax base) the central political questions. These are addressed in chapters 9 and 11, and summarised in chapter 13.

1. Introduction

Figure 4. Agentic AI in municipal daily life: agents working across operations, with people at the centre (illustration).

The idea for this investigation grew during 2025–2026, when there appeared to be no answer to the question of AI's impact on the labour market, nor any published forecasts or scenarios. This is because it is a genuine challenge to build scenarios for business and process development that is not yet established in reality.

The background is the “Visionen” series described in section 0.3. That work showed what is technically possible, but not how large the effect would be on the need for labour. That question is the starting point for this investigation.

The investigation is also an initiative to contribute to the answer to the difficult question that, among others, the Swedish Association of Local Authorities and Regions' (SKR) standing committee on workforce supply, of which Per Högberg is a member, is working on: how is tomorrow's workforce supply to be solved? In its action plan “Kraftsamling för välfärdens kompetensförsörjning” (2024), the committee identified health-promoting factors, full-time work and a longer working life, and early transition, as the areas with the greatest effect (K160). At the committee's retreat in spring 2026, the impact of AI on workforce supply was discussed. The conclusion was that there is a lack of investigations and calculations of this, and that it would be valuable if such an investigation existed, since it could show what awaits Sweden. This is one of the origins of this investigation.

There are no simple answers to tomorrow's workforce supply, since moving people between occupations does not fully resolve the shortfall visible today. The answer being sought is rather: “through changed working methods, fewer people are needed to do what needs to be done”.

Many believe that IT, and not least AI, ought to be able to solve this. To understand that, one must understand what IT has long since done in terms of rationalisation: changed ways of working where machines, in the form of computers and robots – or looms, if we go a little further back in time – carry out parts of our processes.

So what exactly can AI do, and what actually is AI?

AI, artificial intelligence, is systems that interpret what a person wants done and carry it out by combining the knowledge held in the system.

In recent years, services such as ChatGPT have become synonymous with AI. ChatGPT and similar services are built on large language models (LLMs). They have knowledge of language and can ingest reference material, for example a rulebook or a book of law, and answer questions about what it says using ordinary human-like reasoning.

Agentic AI uses a language model to act on what happens. Based on rules and descriptions of how work should be done, the agent can carry out an activity when something occurs. If, for example, a sensor value exceeds a threshold, the agent can retrieve further values, call in staff or other functions, and gather what is needed for a relevant action. The experience can then be used to improve rules, material and models, so that the system becomes better at interpreting events and triggering the right activities. A language model does not, however, learn from itself at each use; the improvement requires that the experience is fed back in.

This can concern simple events, such as a flood, or more complex situations, where a staffing schedule needs to change due to altered workload or sickness. An agent then contacts the people who need to change their working hours, receives the responses, and continues until staffing reaches the level the rules require. But this is also where the human comes in: judging what should happen when the rules cannot be met. Sometimes a person says “we'll probably manage anyway today” – something a computer has no feel for – but with human judgement fed back into the process, the system can get better even at such trade-offs.

Describing the possibilities of AI in a few lines is difficult, but this gives a greatly simplified picture of what has happened and what is to come.

Effects and conclusions

The natural question then becomes: what does this mean?

Three scenarios have been calculated per occupational group: Low, Mid and High.

The time horizon is set at 2035, but it is today impossible to fully calculate when a full effect occurs.

When the shortfall that the Swedish Public Employment Service calculates for 2035, around 270,000 people (AF Analysis 2026:04), has been covered, a surplus remains of around 261,000 people in Low, 595,000 in Mid and 921,000 in High. In Low, a shortfall of around 48,000 people simultaneously remains in care, construction and services, since those freed up are mainly found in other occupations (chapter 6).

Evidence

The effects of agentic AI over the coming ten years cannot be calculated from evidence. The author's assessment is that the breakthrough for agentic AI came during 2026, and it is now that the capabilities are being developed and tested.

The investigation builds, among other things, on what has been measured about the use of language models. How major consulting, technology and software companies view and plan for the development, and the expectations embedded in the large investments in AI, have been included as perspective and as a driver in the High scenario, but not as evidence of effect (section 9.3).

How automation and robotisation have previously changed the labour market has also been included in the material. However, probably never before has such a far-reaching change begun to occur across all types of operations simultaneously. No operation is assessed as unaffected, but the difference between occupational groups is large.

But it is now that we need to understand what the future may look like. This investigation is built on scenarios, not on measured effect.

Anyone using the investigation for labour-market planning in Sweden should therefore help update the calculations on an ongoing basis, preferably every six months, based on the effects gradually becoming visible in the labour market as agentic AI begins to be used widely across occupations.

Conclusion

In the High scenario, the surplus corresponds to around 19% of today's employees and 8.7% of Sweden's population. How the result might transfer to other countries with a similar labour market has not been investigated.

An important conclusion is that mobility between occupations becomes even more important than today: labour is freed up mainly in office, casework and expert occupations, while the shortfall is in care.

If the Mid or High scenario materialises, it is reasonable to test a gradual introduction of shorter working hours in the parts of the labour market where the surplus arises, and not to raise the target retirement age at the pace now following from the rules (sections 11.3–11.4).

2.1 Starting point: the Swedish Public Employment Service's calculation

The investigation's baseline for the shortfall is the Swedish Public Employment Service's (Arbetsförmedlingen, AF) analysis “Labour shortfall and labour from abroad” (AF Analysis 2026:04) with table appendix (K081, K082). In the base scenario, AF calculates an additional labour shortfall in 2035 of around 270,000 people; the table appendix sums to 270,972 across occupational groups. AF itself states a range of 200,000–300,000 depending on assumptions.

The baseline rests on normal economic growth, unchanged matching, full geographic mobility and no mobility between occupations. The shortfall is therefore not a measure of how many jobs stand vacant today, but of how much more labour demand is expected to grow than supply by 2035 if nothing else changes.

The investigation uses 270,972 as its calculation value, since it is the only figure available broken down by occupational group. The range 200,000–300,000 is reported as AF's own uncertainty interval. The break-even figure in chapter 6 (5.6%) becomes 4.1% at 200,000 and 6.2% at 300,000.

What the shortfall measures. AF projects labour demand to 2035 for 147 occupational groups (SSYK at 3-digit level) and each county, based on the National Institute of Economic Research's long-term scenario. Supply is the number employed in 2023 in each occupational group, projected forward with the population aged 20–64. The shortfall is the difference in the occupational groups where demand is greatest. It is counted in people, not full-time positions. It is an additional shortfall: it comes on top of shortfalls that already exist today and are partly handled through labour immigration, and AF assumes today's immigrated labour remains in place (K081, pp. 9–11 and appendix pp. 30–32).

AF's own caveats. AF states that the results are direct model outputs and should be read as indicators of direction and relative differences, not as forecasts. The model does not capture rapid technology shifts, and the baseline is therefore a picture without any AI effect. AF also notes that shortfalls in publicly funded occupations may be overestimated, since productivity there is assumed to grow slowly, and that the occupational groups are heterogeneous (K081, p. 11 and pp. 25–27).

The shortfall under other assumptions. Without mobility between counties, the shortfall becomes around 300,000 people (297,369 when the table appendix's county rows are summed). With full mobility between occupations as well, it becomes around 220,000. If, in addition, all registered jobseekers could be employed in the occupation they are seeking (perfect matching), it becomes 141,473 (K081, p. 27; K082). The difference between 270,972 and 141,473, around 129,500 people, shows how much those currently outside employment could contribute. This is a separate route to workforce supply alongside AI and is addressed in section 11.2.

The choice makes the investigation's calculation conservative: a smaller baseline gives less freed-up capacity. Section 6.5 shows the result using AF's own baseline.

2.4 Sweden in international comparison

Sweden's population growth rests on immigration (K119). The same development, often stronger, is occurring in many other countries:

  • According to the Global Burden of Disease study (GBD 2021, published in The Lancet 2024), fertility is expected to be below replacement level in 155 of 204 countries (76%) by 2050, and in 198 countries (97%) by 2100. Global fertility is expected to fall to 1.8 children per woman by 2050 and 1.6 by 2100; in Western Europe to 1.44 by 2050 (K122).

  • China's population fell in 2025 for the fourth year running, by 3.39 million to 1.405 billion. Births were 7.92 million and deaths 11.31 million. The figures come from China's National Bureau of Statistics, as reported by Caixin (K123). The decline was 0.85 million in 2022, 2.08 million in 2023 and 1.39 million in 2024.

The labour shortfall is therefore not a Swedish peculiarity. The possibility of covering it through immigration is limited by the fact that the same development is occurring in many countries of origin. This makes the question of technology's role an international one, and the investigation's conclusions relevant beyond Sweden as well.

2.5 Other assessments of the need

  • SKR reports, in “Välfärdens kompetensförsörjning 2023–2033” (K121), recruitment needs for municipalities and regions. SKR gives no combined figure; municipalities' need (288,500) and regions' (100,000) sum to around 388,500 (own summation). Recruitment need covers replacement for retirements and is therefore not comparable with AF's shortfall. SKR assesses that elderly-care staffing needs will grow by 65,600 people (+32%, from 204,000 to 270,000), and that half of the increase in the number employed in welfare is needed in elderly care. SKR also shows that shorter working hours increase staffing needs (pp. 48–49).

  • LO (the Swedish Trade Union Confederation) has objected to the picture of a shrinking workforce. LO's working-time inquiry states that the share of the population of working age is expected to be larger in 2050 than today, and that Sweden's demographic development stands out positively compared with countries that have already shortened the working week (K162, p. 7). The objection is consistent with SCB's figures above on the number of people of working age. It is made in a context where LO argues for shorter working hours, and it says nothing about the fact that the need for care is growing faster than the workforce (section 2.3).

The investigation uses AF's assessment because it is the only one that covers the entire labour market by occupation and has a defined method. SKR's assessment shows that care is at the core of the shortfall.

2.6 What is not included in the baseline

  • Defence. The defence sector's planned growth is not included in AF's base scenario in a way that can be separated out. The investigation reports AI efficiency gains in defence in the calculation (type T10) and defence's growth as a separate value outside the model (section 6.9).

  • Green transition. Industrial investments in northern Sweden and electrification could increase labour demand beyond AF's assessment. No verified figure exists in the material.

  • Regional distribution. The calculation is national. AF assumes full geographic mobility. In practice, the shortfall is larger in municipalities with declining and ageing populations, and the investigation's results do not apply directly to any individual municipality or region. Account therefore needs to be taken of the fact that the challenges can be substantial in certain parts of the country, both in terms of labour shortage and labour surplus based on the scenarios in this investigation.

3. Concepts and evidence principles

3.1 Generative AI and agentic AI

Generative AI in chat form (large language models) answers questions, writes and summarises when a person asks it to. The person still carries out the task, but AI makes the writing, summarising and analysis faster. Agentic AI itself plans and carries out tasks in several steps: it retrieves information, analyses it, makes sub-decisions within given rules, uses other systems, and hands over to a person where required. The difference is decisive for the labour market:

Table 3.1 Two ways AI affects work

Augmentation (mainly generative AI) Replacement (mainly agentic AI)
What happens A person becomes faster or better at a task she still performs, for example because AI drafts text or analyses material A process or process step is carried out by an agent; the person approves, handles exceptions and acts on what the agent flags
Effect on need Fewer people needed for the same volume, if the time saved is used for other work The role in the process is no longer needed, wholly or largely
Evidence today Controlled studies exist, with mixed results (chapter 4.5) Essentially only companies' and vendors' own claims (level 1)

Almost all existing measurement concerns augmentation. That no replacement effect is visible today is therefore weak evidence that it will not come. Today's studies, which analyse backward in time, mainly show that work is carried out faster and, in some industries, in greater volume, with roughly the same number of employees (chapter 4). This is the reason the investigation is built on scenarios.

3.2 What AI does in the work: analysis before administration

AI's greatest power lies in analysing large volumes of data and seeing patterns people do not see, and in acting or alerting based on them. Administration and documentation is the part that is easiest to introduce first, since it is rule-based and already digital. But it is not the most important effect. Three steps are linked:

Figure 9. Collection, analysis and action: AI's greatest value lies in the analysis, not just in reducing administration.

1. Collection and documentation. Data is collected automatically, from sensors, systems or conversations, and documented without a person writing it. This frees up time directly.

2. Analysis. The data is analysed continuously and broadly, for a person or an entire operation. Deviations and patterns are detected earlier than with manual assessment.

3. Action. Staff act on what the analysis shows – administering medication, changing an intervention, ordering material, adjusting ventilation, preventing a problem – or the agent itself carries out the next step within given rules.

Examples from care show all three steps. Controlled studies of AI-assisted documentation show that doctors' documentation time decreases, but moderately: in a randomised study at UCLA with 238 doctors, time spent on chart notes fell by 9.5% with one of two tools, while the other showed no statistically significant effect (K151); another study shows 0.36 fewer hours of documentation per day and lower burnout (K152). In a randomised study across 13 US senior-living facilities (171 residents), a sensor system sent automatic alerts to nurses when movement, sleep or gait patterns changed. The comparison group declined functionally faster than the group with sensors; no difference in cost could be demonstrated (K153, level 3). A French pragmatic study of a system for early detection of decline in elderly people at home shows high accuracy (K154, level 2–3).

Other, smaller national pilot projects have shown larger effects, but they are neither documented nor carried out at sufficient scale to be classed as evidence. Isolated claims of 20–30% lower labour input have appeared, but lack a traceable source, sample and measurement method, and are reported here as an unconfirmed indication, not as evidence.

This means that AI in care does not primarily replace physical and relational work. It can free up time for it, and it can reduce the need for care by detecting decline earlier. The second effect – reduced care need through prevention – is not included in the calculation and, if it materialises, would strengthen the result. The same logic applies in many other occupations: caseworkers, economists, engineers and managers are affected mainly through analysis and decision support being produced by AI.

Five rules govern how the investigation draws conclusions:

3.5 Technology generation and historical comparison

Every source states which technology generation it measures: before generative AI (up to 2022), generative AI in chat form (2023–2026) or agentic AI. Most of the Swedish empirical evidence concerns the first two. A source from an earlier generation can show how the labour market has adapted before, but cannot support a forecast for agentic AI.

Agentic AI affects the entire labour market differently from most previous technology changes, since it can be used in almost all processes. The closest historical comparison is electricity, which in the research literature is used as an example of a general-purpose technology. Paul David (1990) showed that it took several decades before electrification showed up in productivity statistics, since factories first had to be rebuilt and work reorganised (K133). Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson (2021) describe the same pattern for AI: productivity may first be measured as lower, since companies invest in a transition that does not yet show up in statistics, and then rise (the “J-curve”, K134). The comparison suggests the effect of agentic AI may be delayed and then arrive quickly.

3.6 Main mechanisms in the calculation

The occupational groups in the calculation are classified by how AI primarily affects the work (main mechanism). Each group can be affected in several ways; the main mechanism indicates what is assessed to weigh most heavily. The occupations below are examples, not a complete list (Appendix A has the full placement).

  • Mechanism 1 – analysis, decision support and casework. AI can carry out large parts of the core task: analyse material, produce draft decisions, process cases and answer questions. Examples: office, casework, expert and managerial occupations.

  • Mechanism 2 – data collection, documentation and analysis supporting physical and relational work. The core work is physical or relational and is carried out by people. AI collects and documents data, analyses it and shows what needs to be done, so more time goes to the core work and interventions can be made earlier. Examples: care, health care, services and parts of the trades.

  • Mechanism 3 – physical automation. Robots or self-driving vehicles carry out the physical work. Requires robotics, not just software. Examples: vehicle drivers, warehousing, cleaning, parts of industry and defence.

  • USA, Stanford (K022). Employment for 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations was, in June 2026, about 19% below the comparison group, mainly through reduced hiring. The study is descriptive; the estimate halves when controlling for education.

Two caveats apply to all these studies. They use exposure measures from the same family, developed for the earlier technology generation, and are therefore less independent than they appear. And the cause of the decline among young people is not established (S3).

4.5 Effects within organisations

Studies of companies and public agencies show time savings for individuals, but small measurable effects at the organisational level. All concern generative AI.

  • Yotzov, Barrero et al., NBER w34836 (K097). About 6,000 managers in the US, UK, Germany and Australia. 69% use AI, 1.5 hours per week on average. Over 90% report no effect on employment and 89% no effect on productivity over the past three years. Managers expect +1.4% productivity and −0.7% employment over the next three years. The methodologically strongest source in the material, but concerns generative AI.

  • Baslandze et al., NBER w34984 (K125). About 750 managers: small aggregate employment effects in the short term; large firms expect to cut staff, small firms expect to grow; routine office roles are declining.

  • UK government evaluation of Microsoft 365 Copilot (K126). 1,000 licences in public administration, autumn 2024. 72% were satisfied and some tasks went faster, but the evaluation found no evidence that the time savings led to higher productivity. Analysis work in Excel became slower and lower quality. The evaluation concerns an early version of Copilot.

  • Dillon, Jaffe, Immorlica & Stanton, NBER 33795 (K127). Randomised trial with 7,137 employees across 66 firms over six months: about two fewer hours of email per week, but the same number of email threads, meetings and documents. The time saved did not translate into more work completed. The authors are Microsoft employees.

  • Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, QJE 2025 (K128). 5,179 customer-service agents: +14% resolved cases per hour on average, +34% for new agents. The study does not address employment, but indicates likely future effects: the largest gains where experience is lowest.

4.6 Why agentic AI cannot yet be substantiated empirically

It is currently not possible to empirically substantiate the effect of agentic AI. The technology has not been in broad use long enough for register data and studies to measure it, and the time from data collection to publication is often 6–18 months. The investigation therefore uses the fragments that exist about generative AI: that it is used to quickly build simple solutions and pilots, for analysis, where language models are strong, and for text and illustrations. These show no massive effect today, since use is at an early stage.

A handful of companies nonetheless show where it can start. The US payments company Block stated in its Q4 2025 shareholder letter (26 February 2026) that it is reducing headcount from just over 10,000 to under 6,000. Over 4,000 people are being asked to leave or enter consultation. The company cites AI tools as the reason (K106). This is the company's own statement (level 1), not an independent measurement. At 50 large banks, the number of employees working with AI has grown by over 25% in a year (K159, level 1–2). The author's assessment is that the effects will likely show up first in financial technology and banking [Assumption].

Since adjustment happens via new hiring, effects show up first among young people and new graduates, in job postings and in relative wages. 5. Method: how the scenarios are calculated

5.1 The question the calculation answers

How much does the need for labour fall by 2035 if the work in each occupational group can be carried out by fewer people with the help of AI, and is that enough to cover the shortfall AF calculates? The calculation gives a level for 2035, not a year-by-year path.

5.2 Step by step

1. Baseline. The number of employees in 2024 per occupational group (SSYK 2012, 2-digit level, 46 groups plus “occupation unknown”), totalling 4,838,418 people aged 16–69 [Source: K062].

2. Shortfall. AF's additional shortfall for 2035 per occupational group, summed from the 3-digit level, totalling 270,972 [Source: K082].

3. Type. Each occupational group is placed in one of ten impact types by main mechanism and assessed impact (table 5.1).

4. Reduced need. The type's percentage for Low, Mid and High is multiplied by the group's baseline [Assumption].

5. Coverage within the group. The part of the reduction that covers the group's own shortfall: the lesser of freed-up capacity and shortfall. What remains of the shortfall is called the remaining shortfall.

6. Surplus. The labour capacity freed up beyond the group's shortfall, and, nationally, that freed up beyond the entire shortfall. The surplus is expressed as a number of employees at today's average working hours.

7. Break-even. The average reduction required to cover the shortfall: shortfall divided by baseline, nationally and per group.

What the percentage includes. The percentage is the reduction actually achieved by 2035. It includes both what the technology can do and how far adoption has progressed. The obstacles in chapter 8 (adoption, regulation, costs, physical automation) are therefore already factored into the levels and should not be deducted again. This is also why Low, Mid and High differ: they describe different speeds of adoption, not different technology [Assumption].

Effect beyond normal productivity growth. AF's demand is based on the National Institute of Economic Research's base scenario, where productivity is already assumed to grow in line with historical patterns (K081, appendix p.30). The investigation's percentages therefore refer to the AI effect that comes on top of that development [Assumption]. It cannot be determined how much of future normal productivity growth will come specifically through AI. To the extent it does, part of the effect is counted twice, and the calculation then overstates what is freed up. The uncertainty is greatest in the private sector; in publicly funded activities AF assumes low productivity growth.

Level of classification. The percentages are set per impact type and applied to 46 occupational groups (SSYK 2). This keeps the number of assumptions down, but a type can contain occupations that differ greatly. Type T5, for example, contains doctors, nurses, assistant nurses and childcare workers, and type T8 contains trades, industry, warehousing and cleaning. Section 6.5 therefore tests the result at AF's own level of detail, 147 occupational groups (SSYK 3), and Appendix B reports both levels.

5.4 Key choices and why

  • No own demand growth (G = 0). AF's shortfall is already based on a growth assessment, and AF's appendix lacks demand by occupational group. Adding own growth on top would risk double-counting. The baseline is therefore today's number of employees. Since demand in 2035 is generally larger than today's employee count in the shortfall occupations, this choice gives a slightly too-low freed-up capacity in precisely those occupations. The author's original growth assumptions are reported as a sensitivity check (section 6.8).

  • Coverage per group instead of a flat matching share. A freed-up caseworker does not automatically become an assistant nurse. By calculating coverage within each group, the calculation shows where the shortfall remains even when the national figure looks good.

  • Break-even as the main measure. Break-even depends only on AF and SCB, not on the assumptions about AI. This makes it possible to state how large an effect is required without claiming how large it will be.

  • Upper bound: no volume compensation in the main calculation. The calculation assumes lower costs do not lead to increased demand. This is the author's main assumption, not an established result (chapter 7). Compensation of 25%, 50% and 70% is reported as a sensitivity check.

  • Exposure measure as support, not rule. The calculation shows AI exposure by occupation (the DAIOE index, Engberg et al.) to support the assessment. Exposure alone did not predict employment change 2023–2024 (K069) and therefore does not drive the percentage.

  • Linear path. Where a time path is needed, the effect is assumed to grow evenly from 2024 to 2035. This is a simplification: real-world diffusion often follows an S-curve and can be delayed (sections 3.5 and 8).

  • Defence kept separate. AI efficiency gains in defence are counted in T10; defence's planned growth is reported separately.

5.5 What the calculation does not do

  • It does not calculate when the effect arrives, only the 2035 level.

  • It does not account for new occupations or new tasks that AI creates; that is addressed in chapter 7.

  • It does not account for early detection via AI reducing the actual need for care (sections 3.2 and 6.6). The calculation contains only the effect of the same care being delivered with less labour input.

  • It assumes no mobility between occupations in the coverage measure. The national surplus measure assumes free mobility. Reality lies between the two.

  • It is national and says nothing about individual municipalities or regions.

  • It does not calculate value capture (who benefits from the savings); that is addressed in chapter 9.

  • It does not account for people currently outside the workforce. The unemployed, underemployed and people far from the labour market can contribute to workforce supply, and AI can help through training, language support and adapted tasks. AF's calculation with perfect matching gives an order of magnitude (section 2.1), but no AI effect can be calculated for it.

  • It does not distinguish between the private and public sectors. The same occupation receives the same percentage regardless of employer, even though the conditions for adoption differ (section 9.5). The results therefore cannot be read as a calculation for individual municipalities and regions.

  • It does not calculate whether the transition is achievable in practice. Whether people can, want to and are able to change tasks, occupation and location depends on education, health, working environment, finances and geography. This is addressed in section 11.2 but is not included in the figures.

6.10 How to read the results

  • The figures are scenarios: what follows if the assumptions in table 5.1 hold. They are not forecasts.

  • The robust result is break-even. That 5.6% is enough in aggregate follows from statistics, not from assumptions. Equally robust is that care's major shortfall occupations require 14–16%.

  • The sensitive result is the distribution. The shortfall sits in occupations where AI mainly supports physical and relational work (mechanism 2), while the freeing-up happens in occupations where AI can take over core tasks (mechanism 1). How much of the shortfall is covered also depends on how finely occupations are classified (section 6.5).

  • The gross figures are an upper bound, since no volume compensation and no new tasks are included.

  • The surplus is freed-up labour capacity, not unemployment (sections 6.2 and 6.11).

6.11 Three states: shortage, imbalance and surplus

The results describe three different states of the labour market. They can coexist, and they require different measures.

  • State A – shortage. Labour is lacking in certain occupations. AI can reduce the shortage. In Low, a shortage remains in care, construction and services.

  • State B – imbalance between occupations. Labour capacity is sufficient in total, but it is freed up in occupations other than those with the shortage. This applies in all three scenarios. The state can persist for a long time, since mobility between occupations takes time and is not always possible.

  • State C – a real surplus. Demand for labour falls even after mobility, new demand and new tasks. Only then do working hours, retirement age and the tax base become the central questions.

The calculation shows states A and B directly. State C does not follow directly from the surplus figures. For the freed-up capacity in Mid and High to become a real surplus, it must not be absorbed by mobility into shortfall occupations, by increased demand (chapter 7), or by new tasks. How much is absorbed cannot be calculated today. The surplus figures are therefore an upper bound for state C, and the measures in sections 11.3–11.6 are conditional on that state actually arising.

7. Compensation and volume effects

Historically, automation has not led to durably fewer jobs in the economy as a whole. Three mechanisms have compensated: lower prices increase demand, savings increase demand in other sectors, and new tasks arise. This chapter goes through what is known about these mechanisms for AI, and why the main calculation nonetheless calculates without compensation.

Figure 21. Three compensating mechanisms and how the investigation handles them.

7.1 Increased demand through lower prices

If AI lowers the cost of a service and the price falls, demand can increase enough that just as many, or more, people are needed. This is determined by the price sensitivity (elasticity) of demand. If demand is inelastic, labour need falls; if it is elastic, it can rise.

  • Nordhaus (2006) estimates the average price elasticity of demand in the US economy at around −0.7, i.e. inelastic, and shows that the employment effect of productivity growth depends precisely on this (K131) [Reference].

  • Autor & Salomons (2018), using data for 18 OECD countries and 28 industries since 1970, find that productivity growth has not reduced employment in the economy as a whole: losses in one's own industry have been offset by gains in other industries, but wages' share of income has fallen (K132) [Reference].

There is no AI-specific estimate of demand's price elasticity by sector. The AI-specific evidence that exists measures volume or employment and gives no support for compensation – if anything the opposite: time savings do not translate into more completed work (K127), job postings in exposed occupations are falling at an increasing rate (K129), and increased machine translation is associated with lower employment growth for translators (K130).

7.2 The author's assessment and how it is used

The author's main assumption is that volume compensation will be low: there is no general volume growth in society able to absorb freed-up labour, and buyers will demand that cost reductions be passed on as lower prices within roughly three years at most, without that in itself creating equivalent new demand [Assumption]. There are signs such demands are already being made: Bain reports that customers of IT services firms are demanding around 20% efficiency gains on contract renewal (K140, level 1). The evidence is not sufficient to determine how large the compensation will be: there is no AI-specific measurement, and historical research shows that demand in other sectors and new tasks have offset earlier automation (K132, K096). The main calculation therefore uses V = 0% as an upper bound, not as a forecast, and 25%, 50% and 70% are reported as sensitivity (section 6.7). In the public sector, volume is governed by need and political decisions, not by prices, and no volume compensation is applied there.

7.3 New tasks and new occupations

Autor et al. (NBER WP 30389) show that most of today's US employment is in job titles that arose after 1940, but also that automation's demand-reducing effect has increased over the past four decades while augmentation's demand-increasing effect has not (K096) [Reference]. History thus shows that new tasks arise, but not that they arise at the same pace as old ones disappear.

New AI-related roles are growing quickly but from a low base. At 50 large banks, the number of employees working with AI has grown by over 25% in a year (K159). This is a gross figure; no source states how many new roles arise per role that disappears. Klarna's rehiring of people (section 4.8) shows that quality shortfalls can create new need for people even after realised replacement.

7.4 Indirect effects and timing

  • General-purpose technologies take time. Electrification only showed up in productivity statistics once work had been reorganised, several decades after the technology existed (K133). Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson show that transition investment can first lower measured productivity before it rises (K134). For agentic AI this means that the absence of effects today says little about the 2035 level, and that effects in other sectors may arrive late.

  • The business cycle as a trigger. Hershbein and Kahn show that US firms carried out routine-saving changes during the 2007–2009 recession, and that they persisted (K026) [Reference]. If this also holds for agentic AI, the effect could arrive earlier than the linear path assumes – for example, in a downturn.

  • Efficiency that does not show up in headcount. Gains in capital tied up, energy or quality do not directly affect staffing need and are not included in the calculation.

7.5 Conclusion

The compensating mechanisms are real but unmeasured for AI. The investigation therefore uses an upper bound (V = 0) and shows how the result changes with compensation through lower prices. The other two mechanisms, demand in other sectors and new tasks, are not calculated. The conclusion that the aggregate effect is enough to cover the shortfall holds even with substantial compensation. The conclusion that care remains the largest shortfall category is not affected by volume compensation, since care specifically is exempt. The remaining shortfall in other occupational groups does, however, change with volume compensation. The conclusion about the size of the surplus is, by contrast, the most uncertain in the investigation: the more that is compensated, the smaller the surplus.

8. Conditions and limiting factors

That the technology exists does not mean it is used. This chapter describes what could make the effect arrive later or turn out smaller than in the scenarios, and what argues for it going faster.

8.1 Adoption is the bottleneck

The consistent pattern in the material the investigation has reviewed is that the hard part is not the technology but getting it into workflows. Among Swedish firms that considered but refrained from AI, 74.7% cite a skills shortage as the reason (SCB, K120). The AI Commission notes that smaller actors struggle to adopt AI (K001), and public administration uses new technology mainly for exploratory purposes (K005). Most firms are at an early stage (K085).

This is not a contradiction of the investigation's assumptions, but it affects the pace. The scenarios state a level for 2035, and the linear path in chapter 5 is a simplification. If adoption is delayed, the effect may arrive later and faster, once workflows have been restructured (section 3.5). The author's assessment is that agentic AI began to be used during 2026; this is a judgement, not a measurement.

8.2 Capability is growing fast

The length of tasks AI models can handle has been doubling at an accelerating rate: every 196 days 2019–2025, every 131 days after 2023, and every 89 days since 2024 (METR, K068). This is a measure of capability (level 1–2), not of use. The exposure measures the evidence in chapter 4 relies on are fixed and show where capability stood when they were produced.

8.3 Cost per unit is falling, use is growing even faster

The price of using AI models has fallen very sharply. According to the Stanford AI Index, in November 2022 it cost around $20 to have a GPT-3.5-level model process a million tokens. By October 2024, the same performance cost 7 cents. What cost 280 kronor therefore cost one krona not quite two years later (K027). Epoch AI shows prices have fallen at different rates for different tasks (K028). Google states that its per-unit cost of running its Gemini models fell by 78% during 2025 (K163, the company's own figure).

At the same time, use is growing even faster than the price is falling. The measures are the company's own (level 2 for Google's services) and show the change over time, not just a level at one point.

The link between falling price and rapidly increasing use matters to the investigation for three reasons. First, a lower price per unit makes more tasks profitable to carry out with AI: tasks not worth automating at the old price become worth it at the new one. Second, agentic AI may require many model calls for a single task, since the agent plans, retrieves information, checks and redoes. Use can therefore grow much faster than the number of users. Third, total spending on compute capacity keeps rising even as the price per unit falls. This is why investment in data centres keeps growing (sections 8.7 and 9.3).

For an individual organisation, cost is hard to predict, since an agentic task can trigger many model calls. Cost uncertainty can make organisations hold back, but cost is not the main obstacle firms cite (K120).

8.4 Physical automation: humanoids and robots

As of mid-2026, humanoid robots are commercially proven only for narrow, repetitive material-handling tasks in structured environments such as warehouses and factories. Epoch AI assesses that robots are typically 3–10 times slower than humans and that the ability to generalise to new tasks is the biggest bottleneck (K039). Tesla's CEO admitted in January 2026 that the company's humanoids were not yet doing productive work (K040). China accounted for around 90% of the world's humanoid shipments in 2025 (K041, secondary source). Several robots are controlled in complex steps by remote operators (K043).

This supports the low assumptions for trades, industry, warehousing and cleaning (T8) and services (T7). Sweden has a high density of industrial robots but no leading humanoid manufacturer.

8.5 Self-driving vehicles

The assumption for vehicle drivers (T9, 20/30/40%) rests entirely on mechanism 3. According to the Swedish Transport Agency, trials of automated vehicles require a permit, and commercial traffic with fully driverless heavy trucks is not yet permitted in Sweden (K117). Regulatory development is ongoing nationally, in Europe and within the EU. The regulatory situation argues for slower rollout in Sweden and Europe than in the US. T9 is therefore the type whose assumption depends most on political decisions. At the same time, adoption could go very fast once the regulations are in place, since the technology will by then already be proven in countries that are further ahead [Assumption].

8.6 Regulation

The EU AI Act sets requirements for high-risk systems, including systems used in recruitment and workforce management. Through Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the so-called Digital Omnibus Regulation, which entered into force on 27 July 2026, the deadlines have been pushed back: requirements for standalone high-risk systems apply from 2 December 2027, and for high-risk systems embedded in products from 2 August 2028 (K138). The new Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 applies from 20 January 2027 and covers machinery with embedded AI (K139). In Sweden, the Post and Telecom Authority is proposed as the main market-surveillance authority (K002).

In general, legislation is not keeping pace with AI development and is not positioned to, since lawmaking is a slower process than the very rapid AI development happening today. The postponement of the EU's requirements is one example. This is also why a debate is under way about temporarily halting or limiting development of the most advanced systems until regulation has caught up: the Future of Life Institute gathered signatures in 2023 for a pause in development of the largest models (K147), and in October 2025 published a call against developing superintelligence before it can be made safe, with over 65,000 signatures (K146). This points to a different challenge than in previous technology shifts. The lack of robust regulation for a technology that is global and spreading very fast through every part of every organisation is a serious problem. It can both slow the pace, through uncertainty and future restrictions, and increase the risks if the technology spreads without control.

It can also create an imbalance between the private and public sectors. The public sector generally follows regulation more carefully than the private sector and may therefore lag in adopting agentic AI, thereby tying up tax funds in labour for longer than necessary [the author's assessment; see also section 9.5].

8.7 Data centres and compute capacity

Use requires compute capacity, and the build-out of data centres has become a political issue in both the US and Europe, since these are large projects affecting the local environment, power grids and land use. The organisation Data Center Watch has mapped local opposition in the US: projects worth $64 billion were blocked or delayed from 2023 to March 2025, and in Q2 2025 opposition concerned projects worth $98 billion (K144). In Ireland, where data centres used 22% of metered electricity in 2024, there was effectively a halt on new connections in the Dublin area 2021–2025, and the energy regulator decided in December 2025 on new conditions requiring data centres to contribute their own capacity (K145).

This creates a risk for adoption: if compute capacity is not built out, the pace could slow. At the same time, data centres compete for electricity with other industry and can crowd it out (section 9.6).

9. Value capture, financing and the tax base

The calculation in chapter 6 shows how much labour can be freed up. It says nothing about where the money goes. This chapter addresses that question. Beyond an illustration of the tax base in section 9.4, no values are set, since there is no basis for them.

9.1 Where the saving goes

When an agent performs work previously done by people, a saving arises for whoever uses the agent. The saving is distributed in four directions:

  • The vendor of the model and platform takes a fee.

  • Customers share in it through lower prices, if competition forces that.

  • The employer retains some as margin.

  • Employees who are replaced share in it only if there is transition support or shorter working hours; the share can be zero.

The distribution determines both the volume effect (chapter 7) and the tax base. The author's assessment is that buyers will, within roughly three years at most, demand that the saving be passed on as lower prices [Assumption]. That argues for the customer share becoming large over time, but not that it creates equivalent new demand for labour.

9.2 The value chain

Spending on AI is spread across several links – energy, chips, networking, cloud services, foundation models, operations and applications – but the margin is concentrated in chips and the largest models. A clear example is Nvidia, the largest supplier of AI chips: in fiscal year 2026 (through January 2026), revenue grew 65% to $215.9 billion, of which $193.7 billion from data centres, with a gross margin of 71.1% for the full year and 75.0% in Q4 (K143, the company's filing with the US financial regulator). The hardware chain is an external dependency for Sweden regardless of where operations run. Local hosting and open models retain more value nationally, but do not directly affect the number of hours worked.

The value chain can be broken into links (table 9.1). It is important to distinguish them, since they differ in where the value is captured and which countries have a realistic position.

This is no reason to forgo domestic applications, but it is a reason not to count on revenue from foundation models landing in Sweden.

9.3 Investment pressure

Investment in AI has no equivalent in previous technology shifts in speed and scale:

  • The largest cloud companies plan investment in AI infrastructure of around $660–725 billion during 2026 (K051, a secondary source compiling the companies' own plans; level 1).

  • Bain assesses that around $1,500 billion per year in infrastructure investment will be required by 2031, and that sustaining it requires a market of around $6,000 billion in annual revenue. Today's use cases – consumer, $200–400 billion, and enterprise, $1,000–1,400 billion – give $1,200–1,800 billion. The rest, around $4,200 billion, must come from new sources. Bain writes that trillions in new revenue are required, not just cost savings (K140, level 1, a forecast from a commercial analysis firm).

  • For comparison: Swedish government spending in 2026 is projected at SEK 1,542 billion (K174). $1,500 billion converts, at the 2025 average rate of SEK 9.82 per dollar (K175), to around SEK 14,700 billion – nearly ten times the Swedish state budget, every year. Global car manufacturing turns over around $2,900 billion per year, according to the analysis firm IBISWorld (K176, a commercial estimate). The annual investment thus equals roughly half of global car manufacturing's turnover, and the revenue Bain says is needed is roughly twice the size of the entire car-manufacturing industry.

This creates strong pressure toward rapid development and adoption: investors need the technology used at scale, and vendors have strong reasons to push adoption in companies and the public sector. This is a reasonable basis for an explicit driver in the High scenario. It is not, however, evidence that the effect materialises. Bain places most of the missing revenue on new services and products rather than on replaced labour hours, so investment pressure alone is not a strong basis for large labour savings. If the revenue fails to materialise, a slowdown with write-downs is a possible outcome. That would lower the pace, but would likely also have large effects on the economy as a whole, since the AI industry has become very large in a short time [the author's assessment].

9.5 The public sector

Different conditions apply in the public sector than in a market:

  • Efficiency gains require investment in systems, data and skills before they produce savings. Municipalities and regions have limited capacity to invest.

  • Volume is governed by need and political decisions. A saving does not become increased sales; it can be used to meet increased needs (which is precisely what the shortfall is about) or to lower costs. This requires politicians to have the skills and insight into what AI can and cannot do, and that skill is largely lacking today.

  • Concern about regulation is greater in the public sector than the private sector. Data protection, confidentiality, the exercise of public authority and the new AI Act mean many operations are holding back. The risk is that tax money is used to retain manual work that could be done with AI, because it is uncertain how AI may and should be used – not because that is best for residents. In a time of labour shortage, this means tax funds are tied up in work that would be needed in care. [Assumption; the author's assessment]

  • If AI leads to unemployment elsewhere in the economy, public costs for income support, labour-market measures and transition assistance rise, while tax revenue falls (section 9.4). These effects are not part of the investigation, but are noted for inclusion in a broader overall assessment.

The public sector thus accounts for both the largest shortfall and a significant share of the risk if the transition does not succeed.

9.6 Crowding-out: electricity, construction, land and skills

AI infrastructure competes for the same scarce resources as other electrification and infrastructure development: stable electrical capacity, construction capacity, grid-adjacent land, and occupational groups such as electricians and grid engineers. Data centres account for around 12% of the Swedish national grid operator's (Svenska kraftnät) current connection requests (per parliamentary interpellation 2025/26:414, K056). Data centres require a great deal of electricity and can crowd out other industry. They are not labour-intensive to operate, and construction jobs are temporary, so they should not be counted as a contribution that solves the shortfall. The debate on data centres in the US and Europe is described in section 8.7.

The priority between market-driven allocation and political direction is a question of values the investigation does not answer.

11. Courses of action

Chapter 6 gives two main results that call for action. First: even when AI frees up enough labour capacity in aggregate, a shortfall remains in care in the Low scenario, since the freeing-up happens in other occupations (states A and B in section 6.11). Second: in Mid and High, capacity beyond the shortfall is freed up equal to 595,000 and 921,000 employees respectively, 12% and 19% of today's employees. If it is not absorbed some other way, a real surplus arises (state C). Sections 11.1–11.2 respond to the shortfall and the imbalance. Sections 11.3–11.6 respond to a surplus and apply only if it materialises. The courses of action are not recommendations throughout; where the author makes a judgement, this is stated.

11.1 Direct AI at the shortfall occupations

The most direct measure against the remaining shortfall is for AI adoption in care, health care and social services to proceed faster than average. This is in line with the AI strategy's goal that Sweden should be the best in the world at AI in public administration (K004). The greatest effect is likely to come from a combination of automatic documentation and ongoing analysis of collected data, so staff can act earlier (section 3.2). Documentation support alone gives a moderate effect according to the controlled studies (section 6.6).

  • State and regional support for shared solutions for municipalities, which individually lack investment capacity.

  • Shared measurement of time savings and care outcomes, so decisions can rest on outcomes rather than vendor claims.

  • Swedish time studies for assistant nurses and nurses, where evidence is lacking.

  • Clearer guidance on how the regulations may be applied, so uncertainty does not become a reason to hold back (section 9.5).

11.2 Mobility between occupations

The second route to the shortfall occupations is for people to change occupation. The author's assessment is that mobility and redeployment of staff between occupations and sectors will become even more important than today. The calculation shows that the freeing-up happens in office, casework and expert occupations, while the shortfall sits largely in care, health care and construction. The transition requires training, often at upper-secondary level (assistant nurse) or higher-education level (nurse), and takes years. It is also limited by the fact that not everyone wants to, or can, move into physically and relationally demanding occupations. The transition-support systems (transition study support, transition organisations) are built for individual transitions, not for moving tens of thousands of people between sectors. The AI Commission points to the need for an active labour-market policy (K001).

SKR's standing committee on workforce supply has, in its action plan “Kraftsamling för välfärdens kompetensförsörjning” (2024), identified three areas with the greatest potential to make use of existing staff's capacity and skills: health-promoting factors, full-time work and a longer working life, and early transition (K160). According to the action plan, the recruitment need in elderly care 2023–2033 could fall by around 8,100 people if part-time workers worked three more hours per week, and by around 15,000 if the average retirement age in elderly care rose from 64.2 to 66 (K160).

These measures are not linked to AI, but they act on the same shortfall the investigation addresses. They are especially needed if developments follow the Low scenario, where a shortfall remains in care. The investigation's reasoning on shorter working hours and the target retirement age in sections 11.3–11.4 concerns the parts of the labour market where a surplus arises, not the shortfall occupations. The investigation is part of the same effort: providing a basis for how workforce supply can be solved when labour is not sufficient.

Those outside the workforce. A third route is for more of those currently unemployed or underemployed to enter the shortfall occupations. AF's own calculation shows the order of magnitude: if all registered jobseekers could be employed in the occupation they are seeking, the 2035 shortfall falls from 270,972 to 141,473 people (K082). AF notes this is not fully realistic, since not all jobseekers have the skills in demand (K081, p.25). AI could ease the path in through training, language support and adapted tasks, but there is no basis for calculating the effect [Assumption].

Is the transition achievable? The calculation shows how large a transition would be needed, not whether it is possible. Someone freed up from an office or casework occupation must be able to, want to, and be allowed to start in another occupation, often involving physical and relational work, different pay and sometimes a different location. Education, health, working environment, financial conditions and geographic mobility determine how many do so. The same applies to employers: the private and public sectors have different conditions for adopting AI and for receiving those who make the transition (section 9.5). This is why state B, imbalance between occupations, can persist for a long time.

11.3 Managing a surplus: working hours

If a real surplus arises (state C in section 6.11) and is to be absorbed without the number employed falling, average working hours could be reduced. The arithmetic is simple: if total demand for hours worked falls by a given share and just as many people are to be employed, average working hours must fall by the same share.

The reverse also applies: in shortfall occupations, a general reduction in working hours increases the shortfall (to do the same work in x% less time requires 1/(1−x) − 1 more people; 10% less time requires 11% more people). The author's assessment: it is reasonable to test a gradual introduction of shorter working hours, as the effects become measurable and in the parts of the labour market where the surplus arises [Assumption]. It cannot be implemented generally in the shortfall occupations until the shortfall is covered, or mobility between occupations works better than today, which is currently a major challenge.

11.4 Managing a surplus: retirement age

The pension system's target retirement age is 67 for those born 1960–1964, i.e. for retirements 2026–2031. The target age is linked to life expectancy and rises by around two-thirds of the increase in remaining life expectancy. The Swedish Pensions Agency's forecast is 68 for those born 1967–1981 and 69 for those born 1982–1997 (K137). Higher retirement ages increase labour supply. In a shortage situation this is desirable; in a surplus situation it reinforces the surplus.

The author's assessment: if developments follow the Mid or High scenario and a real surplus arises, the target age should not be raised at the pace current rules imply, but tested against how the labour market actually develops [Assumption]. The decision should be conditional on the indicators set out in chapter 12. In the Low scenario, a shortfall remains in care, health care, construction and services despite a national surplus of around 5% of employees, and there is then no reason to change the pace.

11.5 Skills and entry for young people

The clearest observed effect so far is that young people in AI-exposed occupations find it harder to enter (chapter 4). If junior roles disappear, the experience needed to review and lead AI-assisted work is threatened (chapter 10). This argues for:

  • Education capacity following how demand actually changes, with particular attention to occupations where AF sees no shortfall while education provision is being expanded;

  • Apprenticeship and trainee schemes where new hires do reviewing work under supervision;

  • Prioritising the monitoring of new graduates' labour-market establishment (Growth Analysis, K083);

  • Recognising that the scale of change the scenarios describe requires new skills-supply strategies across the entire labour market.

11.6 Financing and the tax base

If a larger share of the value accrues to capital and foreign vendors, the labour-based tax base shrinks. In High, the surplus corresponds to employer contributions and income tax of around SEK 250 billion per year, of which employer contributions around SEK 149 billion (chapter 9.4). This is the size of the tax base affected, not a calculated shortfall. On top of this come indirect effects, including on VAT. The investigation makes no proposal and does not address how a shortfall should be handled, but notes that the question needs to be examined before the effect grows large, and that the public sector bears both the largest shortfall and the increased costs if the transition fails.

13. Concluding synthesis

The investigation began with a question from the work on welfare's workforce supply: can agentic AI ease the labour shortage Sweden is heading toward? The answer has three parts.

In aggregate, it is enough. The 2035 shortfall equals 5.6% of today's employees. All three scenarios give a larger reduction in labour demand than that – 11%, 18% and 25%. The first figure follows from statistics. The scenarios are assumptions and should be reviewed as outcomes become measurable.

But the capacity is not freed up where the shortfall is. The shortfall sits in care, health care, construction and services, where the work is physical and relational and AI supports rather than takes over. The capacity is freed up in office, casework and expert occupations. In Low, 73–82% of the shortfall is covered without mobility, depending on how finely occupations are classified, and care's major shortfall occupations do not cover their own shortfall. In Mid, they do.

Sweden can therefore have a shortage and a surplus at the same time. This is the investigation's most important finding. AI changes not just how much work is needed, but where it is needed. Skills shortages in welfare and freed-up capacity in office occupations can coexist for a long time, unless people make the transition and AI is directed at where the shortfall is.

Figure 29. The investigation's thread: shortage and freed-up capacity arise in different parts of the labour market.

This implies an order for policy. First: direct AI at the shortfall occupations, especially care, and measure the effect. Then: make occupational mobility possible at a scale today's systems are not built for, and make use of those currently outside work. Only if the capacity is still not absorbed do working hours, retirement age and the tax base become the central questions. These questions need preparing now, since they take a long time to work through, but the decisions should be conditional on what the monitoring shows.

What the investigation cannot say is how fast this unfolds, how much of the freed-up capacity will be absorbed by new demand and new tasks, and whether the transition is achievable in practice. No one knows that today. The investigation is a first quantitative basis to build on and update, not a final answer.

Appendix E. Glossary

Agentic AI. AI that itself plans and carries out multi-step tasks, within given rules.

Assumption, Source, Reference. Labelling of values in the calculation by origin (chapter 3.4).

Baseline. Number of employees in 2024 by occupational group; the starting point for the calculation.

Break-even. The reduction in labour need, as a percentage of the baseline, required to cover the shortfall.

Shortfall (deficit). AF's calculated difference between labour demand in 2035 and the number employed that would then exist if nothing else changes, in number of people (chapter 2.1).

DAIOE. Index of how exposed occupations are to AI, linked to AI benchmark results.

Substitution. A process is carried out by AI without a human performing it operationally.

Exposure. How large a share of an occupation's tasks AI can, in principle, carry out; says nothing about actual use.

Augmentation. AI makes a person faster or better at a task they still perform.

Freed-up labour capacity. The part of the labour need that disappears when work can be done with less effort; expressed in number of employees.

Main mechanism. The way in which AI is judged to affect an occupation the most (1–3).

Remaining shortfall. The part of an occupational group's shortfall not covered by what is freed up within the group itself.

Low, Mid, High. Three scenarios for how large the effect becomes by 2035: cautious assumption, intermediate, and rapid diffusion.

Level 1/2/3. Classification of evidence: supply/capacity, adoption, outcome.

Impact type. A group of occupations with the same assumed effect (T1–T10).

SSYK. The Swedish Standard Classification of Occupations (SSYK 2012).

Token. The unit in which AI models count text; roughly a fraction of a word.

Volume compensation (V). The share of the reduction offset by increased demand when prices fall.

Surplus. Freed-up labour capacity beyond what is needed to cover the shortfall, expressed in number of employees. Not a number of unemployed people (chapters 6.2 and 6.11).
